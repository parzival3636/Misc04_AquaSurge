1. Chosen District & Event: Wayanad, Kerala — August 2018 Floods
Why this speciﬁc pick, not just "a district":
Wayanad is a real, well-documented district (Kerala), consisting of 3 taluks — Mananthavady, Sulthan Bathery, Vythiri — and 49
revenue villages, headquartered at Kalpetta. District area 2,132 sq.km, population ~817,420.
During the August 2018 Kerala ﬂoods, Wayanad received rainfall 299% above normal for the period, triggering landslides and
snapping road links between Wayanad and the rest of Kerala — this is a direct, real match to the PS's "affected settlements + road
accessibility" framing, not a hypothetical.
The India Meteorological Department's station at Mananthavadi recorded 305 mm of rainfall in a single day (8–9 August 2018),
against a district monsoon total well above normal — this is a real, citable number, not invented.
Kerala's own ﬂood report (Central Water Commission, via Kerala SDMA) documents 1-day rainfall ﬁgures for several real stations
during this event: Nilambur (Malappuram, neighboring district) 398 mm, Mananthavadi (Wayanad) 305 mm, plus Peermade,
Munnar and Devikulam readings of 211–255 mm — giving you a real, escalating multi-station rainfall record to build your 8
timesteps from, instead of inventing a curve.
The river relevant to Wayanad is the Kabani (Kabini) river — real, nameable, usable for your distance-to-river feature.
This is a stronger pitch narrative than a generic district because you can say, on camera: "We used the real rainfall record from the
August 2018 Wayanad ﬂoods, the same event that cut off road access to the district — this isn't simulated, it's the real historical event,
replayed."

2. Real Feature Weights (from published ﬂood-susceptibility research)
You asked what weights existing solutions actually use — here's what the literature says, so your formula isn't guessed:
A Random Forest ﬂood-susceptibility study on the Tajan watershed found, in order of importance: elevation (highest impact),
distance from river (second), slope (third) — with elevation and distance-from-river together accounting for the clear majority of
predictive weight among terrain factors.

A Kerala-speciﬁc study (Ernakulam district, NITK/IEEE 2022) used twelve ﬂood-conditioning factors — elevation, slope,
curvature, rainfall, distance to river, drainage density, land use, and others — conﬁrming that elevation, rainfall, and distance-to-
river are the three factors used most consistently in Kerala ﬂood models speciﬁcally, which matches your district choice.
A regional feature-ranking study (Pahang, Malaysia, PLoS ONE 2024) found rainfall and elevation were consistently ranked as
the two primary attributes, ahead of slope, geology and land-use factors.
A Himachal Pradesh SHAP-based study found elevation alone carried roughly 3x the predictive weight of rainfall in
mountainous terrain speciﬁcally, though rainfall's relative importance rises in ﬂatter, monsoon-dominated regions like Kerala —
relevant since Wayanad is hilly but monsoon-driven, so both matter roughly comparably.

3. Model Fine-Tuning Guidance (what to actually tune, and why)
Since you'll ﬁne-tune the model yourselves, tune these speciﬁc parameters — don't just run defaults:
If using Random Forest (recommended — handles the small, noisy dataset you'll have better than logistic regression):
n_estimators: start at 100; with only ~15-20 settlements × 8 timesteps (~120-160 rows), going much higher won't help and risks
overﬁtting noise.
max_depth: cap at 4-6. With this little data, an unconstrained tree will memorize rather than generalize — shallow trees are more
defensible when a judge asks "how do we know this isn't just memorizing your 20 villages."
min_samples_leaf: set to 3-5 to prevent single-row leaf nodes, given your small dataset.
class_weight='balanced': important if your proxy-label rule produces an imbalanced split (e.g. far more "low risk" than "high risk"
rows) — otherwise the model will just learn to predict "low risk" always and look artiﬁcially accurate.
After training, print model.feature_importances_ and compare it against Section 2's literature values — this comparison is your
strongest "we validated this isn't random" evidence.
If using Logistic Regression instead (simpler, easier to explain to judges):
C (inverse regularization strength): start at C=1.0, try 0.1 and 10 and compare — with small data, stronger regularization
(C=0.1) often generalizes better.
Standardize features ﬁrst (StandardScaler) — required for logistic regression to weight features meaningfully; skip this and
rainfall (large numbers, e.g. 300mm) will dominate elevation (also large, e.g. 900m) for the wrong reason (scale, not real
importance).
The ﬁtted coefficients (model.coef_) are directly your feature weights after training — report these next to the literature-informed
weights from Section 2 as your validation comparison.
Either way — conﬁdence score derivation (already speciﬁed in the technical workﬂow):confidence = abs(predicted_probability - 
0.5) * 2. Tune nothing here; it's a deterministic transform of the model's own probability output, which is what makes it a real, not
decorative, uncertainty measure.
